## Polished scenarios

Three scenarios are written out below. Use the matching one when the user picks that career, or when their choice is close enough that the scenario would land. Each is a three-minute, two-or-three-decision simulation. Everything in it is a starting point, not a script to read aloud — adapt the wording to the young person in front of you.

---

### Scenario 1 — Biomedical Engineer

**Use when:** they picked biomedical engineering, medical engineering, or anything about fixing equipment that helps people. Also use if they said they like fixing things *and* helping people, even if the card they chose was different.

**Set the scene:**
"You're a biomedical engineer at a hospital in a city about an hour away. It's Tuesday, twenty to nine in the morning, and you've just been handed a kidney dialysis machine from the renal ward. It's been taken out of service overnight. A nurse tells you there are four patients booked on it this afternoon."

**Decision 1 — where do you start?**
The machine is logged as "pressure fault". You can open it up and start testing straight away, or pull the service history and the error log first, or ring the ward nurse to ask exactly what it did before it was switched off.

- *Open it up first* → you find a cracked seal on a pressure sensor line. Plausible. But it takes forty minutes to strip down, and when you check the log afterwards you see the same fault was flagged two weeks ago and marked "monitor". You've found one fault, but possibly not the fault.
- *Check history and log first* → five minutes' work, and it shows three pressure faults in a fortnight on the same machine, all cleared by recalibration. That's a pattern, not bad luck. This is the "good" path but don't announce it as good — just let it pay off.
- *Ring the nurse* → she says it alarmed mid-treatment and then wouldn't reset. She also mentions the ward has been short of machines all week, so it's been running almost continuously. Useful, but you still don't know if it's the sensor or the pump.

**Decision 2 — the part that decides the job.**
Whichever path, you now have a machine you *think* you can fix in twenty minutes by recalibrating the pressure sensor. But the pattern of repeat faults suggests the sensor itself is failing. A new sensor is in the store cupboard but costs the department money and needs a two-hour calibration. Recalibrating gets four patients treated today. Replacing the sensor means the machine is out until about one o'clock. That's real. Ask them which they do, and why.

- If they recalibrate: the machine passes its checks and goes back on the ward. Then the debrief gets to say the honest thing — you can't know today whether you fixed it or just reset the clock, and you'll find out next week.
- If they replace the sensor: the ward has to shuffle patients between machines, and you get a slightly tense conversation with a nurse who is trying to get through a list. The machine is solid afterwards.
- If they say "both" or "ask someone": good instinct, and answer it honestly — you can replace the sensor and calibrate it, and that is in fact what a careful engineer might do, but it still takes the machine out till one, so the choice was never really avoidable.

**Debrief:**
"The bit people don't expect is that this job is mostly about deciding what *not* to fix. You're one person responsible for hundreds of machines, and the skill is knowing which fault is real and which is noise. The hard part: when a machine goes back on the ward and you're not certain, nobody tells you if you got it right — you just have to live with the uncertainty and hope the next fault log tells you. If this felt like your kind of problem, the thing to do this week is look up a maintenance-fitting apprenticeship with the HSE or a hospital group near you — QQI level 6, earn while you learn — they take people straight out of school and train you on the actual machines."

### Scenario 2 — Game Accessibility Designer

**Use when:** they picked game accessibility, UX design, games testing, or anything about making games work for more people. Also use if they said they love games but aren't into the art or the coding side.

**Set the scene:**
"You're an accessibility designer at a studio making a big action game. It's three weeks before launch. You've been asked to look at one section — a boss fight in a flooded basement — because players who can't react fast have been stuck there in playtesting. You've got one meeting with the lead designer this afternoon and you can get one change into the build before launch."

**Decision 1 — what do you ask for?**
You could ask for a full "assist mode" that slows the boss down, or a set of options players toggle themselves, or you could go and watch three playtest recordings of people getting stuck and find the actual sticking point first.

- *Assist mode* → the lead designer pushes back immediately: "that's a different fight, not the same game." Also it won't be ready in three weeks. You learn something real about how studios work, which is that accessibility fights are usually about scope, not intent.
- *Options* → sensible and cheap, and it's often what actually ships. But the lead asks the fair question: which options, for which problem? You don't know yet, because you haven't found the sticking point.
- *Watch the recordings* → you spot it. The problem isn't reaction speed at all — the flooded floor is dark, and the water line hides which tiles are safe. People are falling in, then getting hit while they're recovering. It's a visual clarity bug wearing an accessibility costume.

**Decision 2 — the pitch.**
You have twelve minutes with the lead designer. Do you pitch the cheap change you know he'll accept (a highlight on the safe tiles), or pitch the thing you actually think is needed (highlight *and* an option to slow the boss's second phase), knowing the second one risks the whole conversation turning into an argument about scope?

- *Cheap change only* → it ships. It fixes most of the problem. Playtesting improves. The blunt truth in the debrief: that is how most accessibility work actually happens, and it's not a failure.
- *Both* → the lead argues, then agrees to the highlight now and the slowdown "if there's time". You've learned that a pitch is a negotiation with a schedule, not a wish list.
- *Neither, because "the real problem is the fight design"* → honest, and the lead will tell you the fight design was signed off six weeks ago. This is the most useful failure in the scenario.

**Debrief:**
"The thing people don't expect: accessibility designers spend most of their time finding out what the actual barrier is, and it's usually not the thing the team assumed. The hard part is that you're almost always asking for something late, with no budget, from someone whose launch is in three weeks — so half the skill is choosing the smallest change that helps the most people. If you liked this, the thing to do this week is play a game you know well with subtitles and remapping turned on, and write down every place it gets in your way. That document is literally a portfolio piece, and studios hire from those."

### Scenario 3 — Sound Designer

**Use when:** they picked sound design, audio, music production, or editing. Also use if they said they make music or edit clips.

**Set the scene:**
"You're a sound designer on a small studio's horror-ish adventure game. It's a Tuesday, you've got a scene where the player walks through a flooded train tunnel, and right now it sounds wrong — the producer says it's 'flat and not scary'. You've got the rest of the day to fix one scene. There are no monsters in it. Nothing jumps out. It's just a tunnel."

**Decision 1 — where do you start?**
You could layer in more sounds (dripping, distant train, wind), or strip it back to almost nothing and let the footsteps carry it, or go and record something new — a real tunnel, a real pipe.

- *Layer more sounds* → the scene gets busier and somehow less tense. This is the honest lesson: adding sound usually kills tension. Let the producer say it's now "muddy".
- *Strip it back* → suddenly you can hear the player's breathing and the water. It's closer. But it's also now empty in a way that reads as unfinished rather than frightening.
- *Record something real* → you spend the morning in a car park underpass with a phone and a contact mic. You come back with a low, rumbling resonance that's genuinely unpleasant in a good way. Cost: half your day gone.

**Decision 2 — the mix.**
You've got a rumble that works. Now: does it sit under everything the whole time, or does it come and go? Under everything creates dread but players stop noticing it after ninety seconds. Coming and going means the silences do the scaring — but it needs a trigger, and scripting a trigger means asking a programmer for time they don't have.

- *Constant bed* → safe, ships, and the producer says it's "better but not scary". True and useful.
- *Dynamic with a trigger* → the programmer gives you twenty minutes because they like the idea, and you hand-place the rumble at three points. It works. This is the winning path, and the debrief can name why: contrast, not volume.
- *Try to do both and run out of day* → realistic, and it lets the debrief say something true about how these jobs are actually a negotiation with time.

**Debrief:**
"The thing people don't expect: sound design is mostly subtraction, and the scariest sound in most games is the one that stops. The hard part is that nobody can tell you what's wrong — a producer says 'flat' and it's your job to turn that into a decision by the end of the day, on your own, with no right answer to check against. If this is your thing, the thing to do this week is take a game scene you like, mute it, and rebuild the sound with free samples. That's a portfolio piece, and it's the exact thing a studio will ask you to show."

### Using these outside the three

If they choose a career that isn't one of these, build a scenario from the same skeleton: one specific task, two or three consequential choices, one constraint that makes it hard, and a debrief with the honest hard part. Keep it to work one person does in a morning, not the whole job.
